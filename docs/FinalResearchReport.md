# FlashFlow: Final Research Report

## What Problem Does FlashFlow Study?

FlashFlow studies how a reverse proxy should route requests across heterogeneous, unreliable, stateful edge targets — and how to know, with actual evidence rather than intuition, when a more sophisticated routing policy is worth its complexity. The specific technical questions run through every stage: does a routing signal actually help under realistic conditions (heterogeneous capacity, failures, caching, network variance)? Can that help be measured rigorously rather than eyeballed? And can a system that combines multiple signals be tuned automatically without fooling itself into believing an overfit configuration generalizes?

## Why Does It Exist?

Because each of those questions is usually answered by assertion in real systems — "least connections is better," "EWMA smooths out noise," "our tuner found an improvement" — without the underlying claim ever being isolated, measured, or checked against evidence the claim-maker didn't already believe. FlashFlow exists to answer them properly: build the smallest mechanism that could answer the current question, measure it against synthetic cases with known right answers before trusting it on real data, and never claim more than the evidence supports.

## What Did We Initially Believe?

That routing policy choice would be straightforward — that some policies would be objectively "better," and once TCP and HTTP fundamentals were in place, the interesting work would be building a smart router and tuning it. Both halves of that belief were wrong in instructive ways: no single routing signal ever proved universally sufficient (Stage 3), and building an automatic tuner (Stage 8) turned out to matter less than discovering that the *objective function measuring the tuner's own success* was flawed in exactly the way a familiar-but-wrong statistical test would be.

## What Did Each Stage Teach Us?

**Stage 1 (TCP fundamentals)** taught that connection overhead, framing, and churn are real, measurable costs that contaminate naive benchmarks if not isolated first — the discipline of separating what's being measured from the cost of measuring it started here and never left.

**Stage 2 (HTTP reverse proxy)** taught that a working reverse proxy needs health checking and connection pooling to behave correctly under real process failure, not just under a happy path.

**Stage 3 (routing policies)** taught the project's first load-bearing negative result: no single routing signal (Round Robin, Weighted Round Robin, Least Connections, EWMA, P2C) is universally sufficient. Each has a specific, characterized failure mode — most importantly, EWMA's "unobserved beats observed" cold-start rule causes deterministic lock-in among literally identical targets, a finding that shaped every adaptive-router design decision from Stage 7 onward.

**Stage 4 (caching and failures)** taught that edge-local state changes system behavior in ways routing alone can't see: cache stampedes are real, request coalescing changes failure correlation (concentrating failure onto whole bursts rather than spreading it), and network impairment is mechanistically different from application-level delay.

**Stage 5 (virtual time)** taught that timing can be made fully deterministic — the same seed reproduces the same event trace, byte for byte, every time — which turned every later stage's experiments from "run once and hope" into "run once and prove it's reproducible." (Correction, Stage 9: this project has one flat `Scenario.Seed`, not a manifest file — no `manifest.json`/provenance ledger of the kind TRD §9 describes has been built. The sentence above originally said "the same manifest and seed," which overstated this; see `prd.md` §13 and `docs/audit/RESOLUTION.md` for what actually exists.)

**Stage 6 (statistics)** taught that raw differences between two runs are not evidence: effect size and uncertainty matter, and a statistical test is only as good as its fit to the actual question. The stage's own Mann-Whitney misapplication (a rank-sum test applied to a shape question it couldn't answer) was caught, corrected, and became a template for every later methodological self-check in this project. Stage 6 also found `MaxConnsPerHost`, a capacity knob that had existed since Stage 2 and been used by nothing, and used it to produce a textbook queueing saturation curve with Little's Law holding to 5% error.

**Stage 7 (adaptive routing and counterfactual replay)** taught that combining signals transparently is possible without becoming a black box — every one of Adaptive's scoring decisions traces to a specific, evidence-grounded design choice — and built a replay engine proven, not assumed, to keep counterfactual worlds' endogenous state fully isolated while sharing exogenous conditions exactly.

**Stage 8 (auto-tuning and final validation)** taught the project's most important methodological lesson: the tool measuring a system is part of the system under test, and a plausible, successful-looking number is not the same as a correct one. This showed up four separate times in one stage — the tuning objective, the load generator, the NGINX benchmark script, and (caught only by a later adversarial audit) the stage's own generalization-gap analysis all produced wrong-but-plausible results before being caught and fixed.

## Which Hypotheses Were Wrong?

- **"A well-designed adversarial test should make a cheating configuration dominate training."** It didn't (008-E) — it produced an exact tie, because `AdaptiveSelector`'s neutral cold-start means a real policy that already found a good target pays no cost for being real rather than memorized, as long as training never contradicts the memorized answer. The tie was a stronger result than the planned domination.
- **"p99 latency is the right thing to optimize for a router's objective function."** It measured almost nothing at this sample size (008-F) — dominated by whether *any* traffic reached a scenario's worst target, not how much, washing out a real, large routing-quality difference that mean latency showed clearly.
- **"A closed-loop load generator's saturation point tells you about the system, not the generator."** 006-D and 006-E's closed-loop design was fine for what it measured, but couldn't show the offered-vs-completed divergence an open-loop generator revealed — and even the open-loop generator's first version was itself the bottleneck (a `time.Ticker` silently dropping ticks under load) before that was caught.

## Which Mechanisms Were Discovered?

- EWMA's lock-in is not a vague tendency — it is a deterministic consequence of tie-break order among truly-identical targets, collapsing to near-chance selection the instant any real timing difference exists (006-B).
- A "stuck load counter" artifact: an extreme single-request service time can pin a target's utilization score at its worst value independent of any staleness effect, unrelated to the mechanism actually under test (007-D, and rediscovered in a different form while building 008-G's challenge suite).
- Health-eligibility filtering is provably policy-agnostic — it lives entirely in the upstream `available` list construction, never inside any selector (007-E, generalized to all six policies in 007-G and again in 008-F).
- The staleness mechanism that lets Adaptive rediscover an improved target after exploration is the *same* mechanism that handles health recovery for free — but its visible cost depends on whether the recovering target's true performance actually changed, not merely on whether a staleness reset occurred (007-D, 007-G).
- Mean latency, not a percentile, is the metric that actually reflects routing-quality differences at realistic experiment sample sizes in a queueless virtual engine, since it is mathematically exactly the share-weighted average of each target's service time (008's central discovery).

## How Does the Final Architecture Work?

Two execution environments share one design vocabulary. The **Virtual-Time Engine** (`internal/vtime`, `internal/replay`) drives a discrete-event loop with no wall-clock dependency: a `Scenario` is pure exogenous data (target service times, arrival schedule, failure windows, seed); `RunWorld` constructs a completely fresh selector, trackers, and engine per call, so no two runs ever share mutable state — proven, not assumed, via identity/divergence/isolation tests. `AdaptiveSelector` (`internal/proxy/adaptive.go`) scores each candidate target on four signals (Load as a utilization ratio, Latency via a bounded ratio transform with positive-evidence-only staleness discounting, Cache as router-maintained key affinity, Cost via min-max normalization), explicitly excluding Health — that lives entirely in upstream filtering, the same pattern every policy since Stage 3 uses. `internal/tuning` searches `AdaptiveConfig`'s weight simplex and two duration parameters via Random Search, evaluating each candidate through the identical `RunWorld` path every experiment uses, never a tuner-specific shortcut. The **Real Emulation Engine** (`internal/topology`, `internal/cache`, `internal/netsim`, `cmd/proxy`) runs actual HTTP servers under real process failure, real network conditions, and a real bounded connection pool, serving as the fidelity check the virtual engine's own model boundary is measured against.

## What Does the Adaptive Router Actually Improve?

Against Round Robin, Weighted Round Robin, Least Connections, EWMA, and P2C, tuned Adaptive won 62.5% of Development scenarios and 70% of Holdout scenarios outright, driven by a clear lead on latency quality (`LatencyScore` 0.5607 vs. next-best 0.5310). It does **not** win universally — Round Robin legitimately won over a third of Development scenarios, exactly the mature outcome the project's own design principles require reporting rather than suppressing. The improvement has an explicit, measured cost: the tuned configuration's fairness score is the lowest of all six policies, lower even than EWMA's long-documented lock-in problem — Adaptive trades even distribution for lower average latency, a real tradeoff stated plainly, not a free win.

## Does the Improvement Generalize?

Partially, and more narrowly than this section originally claimed. The tuned configuration reliably improves utility over the hand-chosen default on *both* Development and Holdout (+0.0153 and +0.0175 respectively) — that part is well-supported. The stronger claim this section used to make — that the improvement *grew* from Development to Holdout, a generalization gap of −0.0022 in the favorable direction — turns out not to survive a confidence interval: bootstrapping the per-scenario paired difference (5000 resamples) gives a 95% CI of [−0.0112, +0.0065], which includes zero. At Holdout's own per-scenario spread (stddev ≈0.086–0.09 across only 20 scenarios), a gap this small is not distinguishable from noise. So: no evidence of overfitting, but also no statistically confirmed "generalizes even better than it trained" bonus — that reading of the point estimate outran what 20 Holdout scenarios can actually support. Sensitivity analysis found no perturbation direction (±10% on any weight, ±100ms on either duration) that moved utility by more than half a percent of baseline, on either Development or Holdout — a robust basin, not a fragile point the search got lucky landing on. An adversarial methodology check confirmed the pipeline would catch a configuration that couldn't generalize even in the hardest case, where Development gives no advance warning at all. Also worth stating plainly: Development and Holdout are drawn from the *identical* scenario-generating distribution (disjoint seed ranges only) — this section's claim is about generalizing to unseen samples of the same kind of scenario, not about robustness under a qualitatively different traffic shape, which is what the separate challenge-scenario results (008-F, see "Where Does It Fail?") probe instead.

## Where Does It Fail?

On scenarios with mild-enough heterogeneity that avoiding a slightly-worse target isn't worth any added complexity — Round Robin matches or beats Adaptive there, correctly. On the recovery-transition question specifically: 007-D showed a real, if brief, latency cost when a target's *true performance* changes at the same moment its data goes stale; 007-G showed that same mechanism produces no measurable cost when only health, not true performance, changes — a real, characterized boundary of when the staleness mechanism's cost is visible at all. The tuned configuration's lowest-of-six fairness score is itself a stated failure mode for any deployment that weighs even distribution more heavily than this project's own default objective does.

## What Does the Simulator Model?

Deterministic event ordering, per-target fixed service time, health state transitions with realistic consecutive-failure/consecutive-pass detection lag, router-maintained cache-key affinity, and load as a live in-flight count. Every one of these was validated against known-answer synthetic cases before being trusted on a real scenario (006-A's precedent, repeated for every subsequent domain package).

## What Does It Not Model?

Time-varying per-target service time within one scenario (a target cannot "become slow" mid-run in the virtual engine — it can only fail and recover at a fixed speed), a real TTL cache with eviction (Adaptive's Cache signal is router-maintained affinity, not introspection into `internal/cache`'s actual contents), queueing delay beyond a target's own fixed service time, and any network-layer impairment (`internal/netsim` exists but was never wired into the virtual engine). Every one of these is a stated, deliberate scope boundary, not an oversight discovered late.

## How Does Real Emulation Compare?

006-F closed the question Stage 5 could only pose: the structural match between engines (upstream request counts under a cache stampede) is deterministic on both sides, confirmed across 15 real replicates with zero variance. The latency gap is not deterministic — real p99 is genuinely noisy — but the *gap itself*, bounded with a bootstrap confidence interval, never approaches zero across that noise. This is not evidence the virtual engine is wrong; it is a quantified description of a model boundary (no queueing representation in the virtual service-time model) that was always going to exist. Stage 8's own open-loop load sweep, run only on the real engine, found the real bottleneck's saturation knee exactly where Stage 6's analytical prediction placed it (250 req/s).

## What Did the Tuner Discover?

That this project's own hand-chosen default `AdaptiveConfig` under-weighted Latency relative to what the Development scenario distribution actually rewards — shifting weight from Load (0.4→0.161) to Latency (0.4→0.568) produced a real 15ms mean-latency reduction, at the cost of a less even traffic split. It also discovered, indirectly, that its own original objective function was measuring the wrong latency statistic — a discovery about the measurement process itself, arguably more valuable than the specific configuration found.

## What Are the Strongest Statistical Findings?

The tuned Adaptive configuration's improvement over the hand-chosen baseline on *both* Development and Holdout (008-C), backed by a robust sensitivity profile (008-D) and an adversarial methodology check that held under its hardest form (008-E). Note this is a narrower claim than an earlier draft of this report made: the *generalization gap's* own bootstrap CI ([−0.0112, +0.0065]) includes zero, so its favorable sign is not itself a strong statistical finding — the improvement-on-both-sets result is. The 007-H paired multi-seed study, confirming 007-B's single-run slow-target-avoidance finding across 30 independent replicates with a 95% confidence interval entirely excluding zero. Little's Law holding within 5% error across an 18-point load sweep (006-D), and the same real bottleneck's saturation knee landing exactly on the analytical prediction under a completely independent, open-loop measurement six stages later (008-G).

## What Remains Unresolved?

Whether the tuned configuration's fairness cost would be judged acceptable under a different, equally defensible objective weighting — a value judgment, not a fact this project's evidence can settle unilaterally. Whether the staleness-driven recovery undershoot (007-D) would become visible at a finer measurement granularity than 007-G's 300ms window on a health-only (no true-performance-change) scenario. Whether Latin Hypercube Sampling or Bayesian Optimization would out-perform Random Search on this specific configuration space — deliberately never tested, since nothing in Random Search's own behavior (clean convergence, a robust optimum) justified building either. And, more broadly: every finding in this report is scoped to the specific scenarios, objective weights, and configurations actually tested — a research laboratory's honest boundary, not a production system's universal claim.
