# Hypotheses: Experiment 003 — Server-Side Scaling & Routing Policies

## H1 — Round Robin Fairness Under Homogeneous Edges
When all edge targets are configured identically (same simulated processing delay, same capacity), `RoundRobinSelector`'s cyclic, counter-based selection will distribute request volume approximately equally across all healthy edges — independent of concurrency level — because the policy allocates by selection count alone and has no reason to diverge from equal distribution when every target is interchangeable.

- *Prediction*: Across concurrency levels $c \in \{1, 10, 50, 100\}$, per-edge request counts will differ from the ideal even split ($N_{total}/3$) by a small margin attributable to the remainder of $N_{total} \bmod 3$ and ordinary scheduling jitter — not by a systematic skew toward any one edge.
- *Corollary*: Because Round Robin carries no notion of target state, this fairness property should hold regardless of concurrency, and is expected to be a property of the *selector's counting logic*, not of the runtime traffic conditions. This experiment is a **necessary baseline**, not evidence that Round Robin is a good general-purpose policy — Experiment 002-D already demonstrated Round Robin's failure mode (blind equal distribution collapses throughput when edges are *not* homogeneous). H1 only establishes that RR does what it claims to do (equal counts) when its core assumption (homogeneity) actually holds.

## H2 — Weighted Round Robin Under Known, Static Heterogeneous Capacity
When edge capacity is heterogeneous but the *ratio* is known in advance and encoded as static weights (weight inversely proportional to expected service time), `WeightedRoundRobinSelector` will recover most of the throughput and tail latency that Experiment 002-D showed Round Robin loses — because the vast majority of traffic will be steered away from the slow edge, rather than one-third of it being blindly sent there regardless of consequence.

- *Setup*: Reuses Experiment 002-D's exact topology and concurrency (edge-a=1ms, edge-b=1ms, edge-c=100ms, c=30, r=600) so results are directly comparable to a prior, already-published finding rather than an unrelated new baseline.
- *Prediction*: A weight configuration proportional to service *rate* (roughly 100:100:1 for edges at 1ms:1ms:100ms) will substantially outperform both plain Round Robin and an equal-weight (1:1:1) WRR configuration — the latter included as a sanity check that WRR degenerates to RR-like behavior when given no information, not because it is expected to help on its own.
- *Explicit limitation this experiment does NOT resolve*: static weights only work if the configured ratio is correct and the underlying capacity difference is stable. This experiment deliberately does not test what happens when an edge's true latency changes *after* weights are configured — that is the motivating question for Least Connections (Experiment 003-C), not this one.

## H3 — Least Connections Discovers Load Without Being Told the Ratio, and Adapts When Capacity Changes
`LeastConnectionsSelector` tracks application-level in-flight request count per edge (`internal/proxy/load_tracker.go`), not a configured weight. Under sustained concurrent traffic, a slower edge holds requests "in flight" for longer, so — by an argument structurally similar to Little's Law ($L = \lambda W$: for a fixed per-edge arrival rate $\lambda$, the average number in flight $L$ scales with service time $W$) — a slow edge's in-flight count should rise relative to fast edges purely as an emergent consequence of it taking longer to finish each request, with no configuration telling the selector this in advance.

This experiment tests three distinct, separable predictions:

- *H3a (matches static config without being told the ratio)*: Under the same static 1ms/1ms/100ms topology as Experiment 002-D/003-B, Least Connections should recover throughput and tail latency comparable to (not necessarily better than) 003-B's *correctly hand-configured* WRR — without ever being given the 100:1 capacity ratio.
- *H3b (adapts to changing capacity; the specific limitation H2 identified)*: When an edge that was fast when weights/selection began later becomes slow (simulating real capacity change after configuration), Round Robin and a WRR frozen at its original 1:1:1 configuration will continue routing to it as if nothing changed, while Least Connections — because it reacts to the resulting rise in the edge's in-flight count, not to a stale config value — should measurably shift traffic away from it without any reconfiguration.
- *H3c (a predicted limitation, not a strength)*: Least Connections' signal depends on concurrent requests overlapping in time long enough for a slow edge's in-flight count to visibly diverge from fast edges'. At concurrency = 1 (no overlapping requests, ever), Least Connections should show **no measurable advantage** over Round Robin on the same heterogeneous topology, because there is never more than one request in flight anywhere to compare. This is not a bug — it is the mechanism's precondition made visible, and it should be tested rather than assumed away.

## H4 — Latency-Aware EWMA Reacts to Measured Response Time, With a Reactivity/Stability Trade-off
`EWMASelector` (`internal/proxy/ewma.go`) tracks a smoothed estimate of each target's recent upstream round-trip latency (`internal/proxy/latency_tracker.go`), updated only on successful round trips, with a cold-start rule that forces trying every target once before trusting any estimate (a direct response to Experiment 003-C's H3c finding about sustained ties).

- *H4a (matches or exceeds LC on the static case)*: Under the same static 1ms/1ms/100ms topology as 003-B/003-C, EWMA (default alpha=0.2) should perform comparably to Least Connections — both are dynamic, config-free policies discovering the same underlying capacity difference through different signals (in-flight count vs. observed latency).
- *H4b (adapts to changing capacity, like LC)*: Under the same mid-run degradation scenario as H3b, EWMA should shift traffic away from an edge that becomes slow after the run starts, without reconfiguration — because it is reacting to measured response time directly, not a stale assumption.
- *H4c (the reactivity/stability trade-off — not yet answered by any experiment so far)*: Under a topology where one edge's latency **oscillates** between fast and slow repeatedly during a run, a low alpha (slow-adapting, e.g. 0.05) should lag behind each change — continuing to send meaningful traffic to an edge for a while after it has become slow, and continuing to under-favor it for a while after it recovers — while a high alpha (fast-adapting, e.g. 0.6) should track each change more quickly but is expected to be more volatile. *Prediction is directional only*: this experiment exists specifically because the master brief's question ("is EWMA too reactive or too slow?") does not have an a priori answer — it is an empirical question about a real trade-off, not a hypothesis with a known correct resolution.

## H5 — Power of Two Choices Fixes Lock-In Among Equal Targets, But Not All Staleness
`P2CSelector` (`internal/proxy/p2c.go`) samples two candidates uniformly at random on every selection (explicit, injected, seeded `*rand.Rand`, per the project's reproducible-randomness requirement) and picks the better by a pluggable score — either `ScorerFromLoad` (in-flight count) or `ScorerFromLatency` (EWMA estimate), reusing the trackers already built for Least Connections and EWMA rather than introducing a third kind of per-target state.

Unit testing this selector directly (`internal/proxy/p2c_test.go`) already surfaced a precise, important boundary on the fix, discovered while writing a test that initially assumed something false: P2C only ever records an observation for the target it actually dispatches to (the winner of a sampled pair), never the loser. This means:
- *H5a (fixes the specific 003-D lock-in)*: among genuinely equal targets, P2C should NOT collapse to one dominant target the way EWMA's full-scan argmin did (Experiment 003-D's pure-homogeneous check) — because random pairing means an equally-good rival keeps winning roughly half of the comparisons it happens to be sampled into, keeping its own estimate fresh, rather than being frozen out the moment a noisy early sample favors its rival.
- *H5b (does not fix recovery-detection for a latency-scored, genuinely-losing target)*: a target that is deterministically worse than its rivals still never wins a comparison under P2C, so it is never dispatched and never re-observed — if its true latency later improves, `ScorerFromLatency`-based P2C has no way to discover this, the same blind spot EWMA has (Experiment 003-D H4c).
- *H5c (load-scored P2C does not share H5b's blind spot)*: `ScorerFromLoad` reads live in-flight count, not a memory of a past sample — an idle target's score is always truthfully 0 regardless of how long it has been idle, so a load-scored P2C selector should correctly treat a recovered (or simply currently-idle) target as immediately eligible again, unlike the latency-scored case.

This experiment tests H5a/b/c against real HTTP infrastructure (not just the unit-level proof already established), and adds one scenario no prior Stage 3 experiment ran: **degradation followed by recovery** — every previous dynamic scenario (003-C H3b, 003-D H4b) only tested an edge getting worse, never getting better again afterward, so the recovery-detection question (H5b vs H5c) has not actually been observed in a live topology until now.

## H6 — Comparison Matrix: Burst and Failure (the two scenarios not yet covered)

Experiments 003-A through 003-E already cover homogeneous, static-heterogeneous, dynamic-degradation, low-concurrency, and degradation-then-recovery scenarios across all five policies (Round Robin, Weighted Round Robin, Least Connections, EWMA, P2C). Two scenarios named in the Stage 3 brief's comparison-matrix template have not been tested with all five policies yet: a sudden load **burst**, and a hard edge **failure** (not merely degraded — actually unreachable). This experiment fills those two gaps and then synthesizes all six experiments (003-A–F) into one final comparison, rather than re-running already-controlled scenarios for their own sake.

- *H6a (burst)*: Under a topology already known to break Round Robin (1ms/1ms/100ms), a sudden concurrency spike (baseline c=10 → burst c=150 → cooldown c=10) should stress the state-aware policies' known race windows (documented on `LoadTracker.Get` and `LatencyTracker`'s read-then-write pattern) more than at the moderate c=30 used throughout 003-B–E. *Prediction is directional only*: whether this materially degrades routing quality at higher concurrency, or whether the policies remain robust, is an open empirical question, not a foregone conclusion.
- *H6b (failure)*: On a homogeneous 3-edge topology (isolating "failure re-routing quality" from the heterogeneity questions already covered elsewhere), hard-killing one edge mid-run should produce a brief error spike bounded by the health prober's detection interval, followed by full exclusion from `available` for every policy — because health-eligibility filtering happens identically upstream of every `TargetSelector` (`internal/proxy/proxy.go`: `available` is built from `registry.IsAvailable` before any selector is consulted). The specific research question is whether the five policies differ meaningfully in how they redistribute the surviving traffic across the two remaining edges once the dead one is excluded, or whether failure handling is effectively policy-agnostic because it happens entirely upstream of routing.
