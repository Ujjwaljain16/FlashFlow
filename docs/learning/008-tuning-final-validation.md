# Stage 8 Learning Notes — Auto-Tuning, Experiment Operations & Final Validation

## Before Stage 8

Stage 7 built a six-signal adaptive router whose every scoring decision traces to a specific, evidence-grounded design choice, and a counterfactual replay engine proven — not assumed — to separate exogenous conditions from independently-evolving endogenous state. Both were individually validated and both produced real, statistically-supported findings. What Stage 7 explicitly deferred was the final engineering question this whole project's progression had been building toward: can FlashFlow automatically search the adaptive router's configuration space, and can it tell whether whatever it finds actually generalizes — rather than merely winning the scenarios it was tuned on?

## The Central Question

*Can FlashFlow automatically find a useful adaptive-routing configuration without overfitting to the scenarios used during tuning?* The word that matters is **generalization**. A tuner that wins its training scenarios and loses on unseen ones is not a success, no matter how sophisticated the algorithm behind it.

## Scope Discipline

Random Search was built first and, on the evidence gathered, is where the stage stopped — no Latin Hypercube Sampling, no Bayesian Optimization. The search space (200 evaluations over a 3-dimensional weight simplex plus two durations) converged well before exhausting its budget and plateaued cleanly; nothing in that behavior justified the added complexity of a smarter algorithm. The tunable parameter set is exactly `AdaptiveWeights`' four fields plus `ReferenceLatency`/`StaleAfter` — the parameters the actual Stage 7 implementation exposes, not an invented set designed to look more sophisticated. The scenario generator is bounded to what `internal/replay.Scenario` can actually execute (target count, service-time heterogeneity, arrival timing, one optional failure window) — no cache/TTL or network-impairment dimensions, since the virtual engine doesn't model either.

## A Real Redundancy, Found and Removed

Before any search ran, `internal/tuning/space.go`'s design rested on a specific mathematical claim: `AdaptiveSelector.SelectTarget` picks an argmax across candidates, and scaling all four weights by a positive constant multiplies every candidate's score by that same constant, which cannot change the argmax. Two weight vectors differing only by scale are therefore behaviorally identical. This wasn't just argued — it was checked empirically (008-A): a weight vector scaled by 7x, invalid under the space's own sum-to-1 rule, produced `Scores` identical to the last digit against the unscaled default when actually evaluated. Searching the 3-dimensional simplex instead of the unconstrained 4D non-negative space wasn't a convenience; it eliminated a confirmed redundancy in the search space.

## The Central Methodology Discovery: p99 Was the Wrong Metric

The most consequential finding of this stage was not about the router — it was about the stage's own objective function, caught while building the final policy comparison (008-F), which returned Round Robin beating tuned Adaptive on 90% of Development scenarios, flatly contradicting every finding since Stage 3. Investigated rather than accepted: `LatencyScore` was built from `P99LatencyMs`, and at 300 requests per scenario, p99 sits at roughly the 3rd-worst sample — so as long as *any* policy sends even a handful of requests to a scenario's single worst target (true of every policy tested, via cold-start exploration alone), p99 lands on that target's raw service time regardless of how much other traffic was correctly steered away. Direct evidence: one scenario showed Adaptive's median latency at 52ms against Round Robin's 130ms — a large, real, mechanistically obvious difference — while both policies' p99 was identical at 166.5ms. With the 0.6-weighted latency term contributing almost no signal, the 0.1-weighted `FairnessScore` (which inherently rewards Round Robin's even split, and *penalizes* the very traffic concentration a good router should produce) ended up deciding the ranking. The fix: since this replay engine has no queueing model, mean latency across a scenario is mathematically exactly the share-weighted average of each target's service time — precisely the quantity routing quality should be judged on. 008-B through 008-E were all re-run under the corrected objective; every qualitative conclusion held, several (008-B's improvement, from 1.22% to 2.37%) more strongly than before. This is the same class of mistake as 006-C's Mann-Whitney misapplication: a familiar, well-regarded statistic applied to a question it doesn't actually answer at the sample size in play.

## Tuning Results

Random Search (200 evaluations, optimizer seed independent of every scenario's own exogenous seed) found a configuration emphasizing Latency far more than the hand-chosen default (weight 0.568 vs 0.4) and de-emphasizing Load (0.161 vs 0.4), improving Development utility by 2.37% through a genuine 15ms mean-latency reduction. Evaluated against Holdout for the first and only time (008-C), the improvement nominally *grew* (+0.0175 vs +0.0153 on Development) — a negative generalization gap, the "training=good, holdout=good" pattern that would be the stronger research result if it survived scrutiny. **It only partly did.** An adversarial audit, conducted after this document's first draft, pointed out that the −0.0022 gap had never been given a confidence interval — a real inconsistency with this project's own statistical discipline since Stage 6. Bootstrapping it (per-scenario paired dev-minus-holdout differences, `BootstrapDiffCI`, 5000 resamples) gives a 95% CI of **[−0.0112, +0.0065] — it includes zero**. At Holdout's per-scenario stddev (≈0.086–0.09), a gap of −0.0022 simply isn't resolvable from noise with only 20 Holdout scenarios. The defensible claim is narrower than what was first written: the tuned configuration improves utility on both Development and Holdout (well-supported), not that its improvement demonstrably grows on Holdout relative to Development (not statistically supported). Perturbing every one of the winner's 6 parameters by ±10% (weights) or ±100ms (durations) moved utility by under 0.5% of baseline in every case (008-D) — a robust basin, not a fragile knife-edge the search got lucky landing on.

Two further scope points the audit surfaced: Development and Holdout share the identical `ScenarioSpace` distribution (same target-count/service-time/failure-probability ranges), differing only by seed range — so this is a same-distribution generalization check, not a distribution-shift one (008-F's hand-crafted challenges cover that instead). And Holdout was technically evaluated twice across the stage (once under the original, flawed p99-based objective, again here under the corrected one) — worth recording explicitly per the master context's holdout-contamination rule, though the correction itself was driven entirely by Development-side evidence, never by inspecting Holdout first.

## The Adversarial Test That Corrected Its Own Prediction

008-E set out to construct a case where a memorization-based "cheater" configuration dominates training but fails on Holdout — the master context's own rule-40 example. It didn't produce strict domination; it produced an exact tie. Investigating why revealed something sharper than the planned result: `AdaptiveSelector`'s neutral (not optimistic) cold-start means a real signal-based policy that has already found a genuinely good target has no reason to explore away from it, so it pays *zero* cost for being real rather than memorized, as long as nothing in Development ever contradicts the memorized answer. Development performance alone gave literally no signal to distinguish the two configurations. On Holdout, where the fast/slow assignment was swapped, the tie broke catastrophically (0.842 vs 0.600) — confirming the pipeline's Development-then-Holdout ordering catches this even in the harder case where training shows no suspicious signal whatsoever.

## Final Policy Evaluation

Once the objective was corrected, the comparison against Round Robin, Weighted Round Robin (given the single most favorable case it can be given — perfect per-scenario profiling), Least Connections, EWMA, and P2C told a coherent story matching Stage 3 through Stage 7's accumulated evidence: tuned Adaptive had the clear highest `LatencyScore` of the six (0.5607 vs next-best 0.5310) and won 62.5%/70% of Development/Holdout scenarios outright — not universal dominance, which the master context explicitly says is the honest, expected outcome (rule 43). Round Robin legitimately won 35% of Development scenarios; that is reported plainly, not suppressed. The tuned configuration's cost is real and stated explicitly: its `FairnessScore` (0.2296) is the *lowest* of all six policies, even below EWMA's long-established lock-in problem — the tuner traded fairness for latency, a genuine multi-objective tradeoff (rule 8) rather than a free improvement.

## The Permanent Challenge Suite

`internal/challenge` fills the gaps a survey found in existing coverage (the cache-hit-never-reaches-upstream invariant, for instance, was already permanently tested at the real-engine level and was not duplicated) across routing, health, cache, network, replay, and tuning. Two of its own tests failed on first attempts for real, diagnosable reasons: a load-counter confound identical in shape to 007-D's (arrival spacing shorter than service time caused both targets to oscillate regardless of the intended health intervention), and a detection-latency mismatch (a 50ms outage recovered before the health registry's required second consecutive probe failure, 100ms later, could ever fire). Both are now documented, permanent regression anchors rather than quietly-fixed parameters.

## Performance Benchmarks and the Open-Loop Load Sweep

Go micro-benchmarks placed routing decision cost in clear perspective: Round Robin at 4.4ns, Adaptive's full multi-signal computation at 259ns — both negligible next to any real network latency. The load sweep closed a real gap: 006-D and 006-E were both closed-loop generators (a fixed worker pool, each worker blocking on the previous response), which by construction cannot let offered load exceed completed capacity. A new open-loop generator, sweeping 20-600 req/s against the identical real bottleneck (`MaxConnsPerHost=5`, 20ms Origin delay, analytical ceiling 250 req/s), needed two of its own corrections before its result could be trusted: a shared `time.Ticker` was silently dropping ticks under load, making the generator itself the bottleneck rather than Origin (fixed by giving each request an independent absolute-time sleep instead of a shared channel); and the planned knee-detection metric (throughput/offered ratio) never triggered, because a generous timeout meant nothing was ever dropped for that ratio to detect — latency inflection was the metric that actually carried the signal, and once used, the observed knee landed at exactly 250 req/s, precisely matching the analytical prediction.

## The NGINX Reference Benchmark's Own Lesson

A first run of the minimal NGINX comparison (rule 55) reported NGINX at ~2ms mean latency — faster than Origin's own 20ms artificial delay could possibly allow if genuinely proxied through. That impossibility was the tell: a Windows/Git-Bash path-mangling bug had silently broken the Docker volume mount, leaving NGINX serving its own default welcome page — a perfectly valid HTTP 200, just with completely wrong content, a class of bug no status-code or error-count check can catch. Fixed with `MSYS_NO_PATHCONV=1` and a permanent content-verification step added to the benchmark script.

A second issue surfaced later, not by re-running the benchmark but by an adversarial audit asking whether "identical configuration" was actually true: `cmd/proxy` was left at its default `-debug-headers=true`, adding `X-Request-ID`/`X-Selected-Edge`/`X-Upstream-Latency` response headers (and the small cost of computing them) that NGINX's plain passthrough never carries — so response bytes were not byte-for-byte identical as the comparison had claimed. The same audit noted the benchmark client had no explicit warm-up phase and relied on Go's default `MaxIdleConnsPerHost=2`, forcing connection churn under concurrency 10 for both systems equally (not a bias, but not steady-state either). Fixed: `-debug-headers=false` in the script, plus a `-warmup` flag and matched connection-pool sizing added to `cmd/experiment-008h`. The corrected result (FlashFlow proxy 21.1ms mean, NGINX 25.4ms, both close to Origin's baseline) is reported as exactly what it is: a single, light-load reference point, not a claim about which system is "better" — and a second reminder, on top of the path-mangling bug, that a benchmark's own configuration needs the same scrutiny as the system it's measuring.

## Surprises

1. **The objective function's own flaw was the single most consequential discovery of the stage** — not a routing finding, a measurement-methodology one, exactly the same category of lesson 006-C already taught with Mann-Whitney.
2. **008-E's adversarial construction produced a tie instead of the planned domination, and the tie was the more informative result** — Development gave zero signal to distinguish a real policy from a lucky memorizer, a sharper demonstration of why Holdout matters than strict domination would have been.
3. **Every load-generator correction in this stage (the ticker-drop bug, the knee-metric fix, the NGINX path-mangling bug) shared one root cause**: the tool measuring the system was itself part of the system under test, and each one produced a plausible-looking, successful-seeming number that was wrong.
4. **The tuner's real improvement came entirely from re-weighting Latency over Load, at an explicit fairness cost** — an honest multi-objective tradeoff, not a free lunch, visible only once the objective itself measured the right thing.
5. **The pattern in Surprise 3 extended to this document's own analysis, not just its load-generation tools.** An adversarial audit (conducted after this stage's first draft closed) found that the generalization gap's headline number had no confidence interval, and that the NGINX benchmark's "identical configuration" claim wasn't actually true (`cmd/proxy`'s debug headers were left on). Both were plausible-looking, successful-seeming claims that didn't hold up under the same scrutiny this project has applied to everything else since 006-C — a reminder that self-auditing a stage's own headline claims is not optional busywork, it is where this exact class of error keeps hiding.

## Limitations

- All Adaptive findings use `DefaultObjectiveWeights` (Latency 0.6, Reject 0.3, Fairness 0.1) — an explicit, stated evaluation preference (rule 8), not a claim of scientific optimality. A different weighting could reasonably favor a different final configuration.
- Holdout is a same-distribution sample of Development's own `ScenarioSpace` (disjoint seed range, identical generation parameters), not a distributionally different test set — it rules out overfitting to the specific 40 training scenarios, not robustness under a qualitatively different traffic shape.
- Only Random Search was built. Latin Hypercube Sampling and Bayesian Optimization remain unbuilt, correctly, per the master context's own instruction not to add sophistication the evidence hasn't justified.
- The permanent challenge suite's scenario coverage is deliberately not exhaustive against every bullet the master context lists as an example (a true mid-scenario "best target reverses" case remains unimplementable without extending `internal/replay.Scenario` for time-varying service time, a capability no Stage 8 finding required building).
- The NGINX reference benchmark is a single light-load run with no statistical replication, deliberately — that level of rigor is this project's standard for its own scientific claims, not for a narrow, explicitly-caveated external reference point.
- CPU/memory instrumentation in the load sweep reports process-level heap and peak goroutine count, not a true CPU-utilization profile (Go has no portable, dependency-free way to read that without platform-specific syscalls) — an honestly-stated scope boundary, not a claim of complete resource profiling.

## Evidence Discipline

**Strong** (deterministic, replicated, mechanism identified): the weight-simplex scale-invariance (008-A, checked empirically); the tuned configuration's *improvement on both Development and Holdout* and its robust sensitivity profile (008-C, 008-D); the adversarial methodology check (008-E); health eligibility holding with zero exceptions across all six policies (008-F, generalizing 007-E/007-G once more); the load sweep's knee landing exactly at the analytical prediction (008-G). **Suggestive, precisely characterized**: the final policy comparison's win rates (008-F) — real and mechanistically explained, but from one Development/Holdout split, not yet replicated across independently-generated splits the way 007-H replicated a single-scenario finding; and, downgraded here from an earlier "strong" tiering, the *negative generalization gap itself* (008-C) — its 95% bootstrap CI is [−0.0112, +0.0065], which includes zero, so the gap's favorable sign is not distinguishable from noise at Holdout's 20-scenario size, even though the underlying improvement on both sets is well-supported. **Unresolved, explicitly flagged**: whether the tuned configuration's fairness cost would be judged acceptable under a different, equally defensible set of objective weights — a genuine value judgment this stage's evidence cannot settle on its own.
